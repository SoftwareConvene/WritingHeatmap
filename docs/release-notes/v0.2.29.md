**Update:** download `writing-heatmap-extension-v0.2.29.zip` below, unzip it, and update the extension the same way as before.

**Quick check: the whole class at a glance**
- **Same section in every document** now opens in **Quick check**: one thin row per student with their name, a colour bar for the ticked section(s), the word count, how many words were added all at once or typed beside a deleted paste, and the review status.
- Click a row to read that student's section; click it again to close it. **Open all** and **Close all** do every row at once, and **Next ↓** / **↑ Previous** open the next student and close the last.
- **Sort** the rows by class list order, most added all at once, most revising, or fewest words.
- **Full text** shows every student's section one after another, as before.

**Opening the side panel from the Doc**
- The **Writing Heatmap** button in the bottom-left corner of a Doc now opens the side panel beside the Doc, with a short summary of each student. The **↗** next to it opens the full view in a new tab, as the button did before.
- The setup guide now starts its tips with how to pin the extension's icon (the puzzle piece at the top right of Chrome → the pin next to Writing Heatmap). The pinned icon opens the same side panel.

**Plainer wording**
- The colours have plainer names everywhere: **Written straight through** (yellow), **Some revising** (blue), **Major revisions** (green), **Added all at once** (red), and **Mixed**. The class table's columns use the same names.
- The Passage box says "Written by Student 3" instead of "Written by: Editor 2 (Student 3)", and its facts are in plain words: length in characters, how many characters were deleted or replaced afterwards, and how much was typed in order.
- The Summary reads "Time spent writing", "Days worked on", "People who edited" and "Biggest piece added at once", with each colour's share given as "…of students' text".
- The notice at the top of each document now reads: "This shows how the text got into the document. It can’t tell you who was at the keyboard or why, so use it to start a conversation with the student, not as proof on its own." The notice about pastes is plainer too.
